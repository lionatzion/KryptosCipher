Enthusiasts have long speculated that the physical properties of the Kryptos sculpture—raised letters, mis‑aligned characters, the S‑shaped copper screen and even the Morse‑code messages carved into the nearby granite blocks—may contain meta‑instructions for solving the fourth passage.  There are, however, only a few publicly available discussions of how to turn those physical clues into a systematic decryption strategy, and most are speculative rather than formal research.
	•	Mis‑aligned letters and Fibonacci ideas.  A small community of bloggers notes that several letters in the K3 plaintext are raised above the copper surface and spell “ENDYAHR”.  One blog post argues that these raised letters, combined with the deliberate misspellings in K1–K3, could form a Fibonacci or reverse Fibonacci sequence that hints at the encryption algorithm .  The author suggests the pattern might indicate a “reverse” cipher or point to other anomalies like the backward Vigenère table and the related Antipodes sculpture .  This idea is interesting but remains untested and has not been endorsed by Sanborn or Scheidt.
	•	Raised letters and positional analysis.  Some cryptanalysis discussions point out that the raised letters Y, A and R in the K3 section are each elevated about 1 cm and may be intended to align with specific rows or columns when the K4 text is overlaid on the right‑hand Vigenère tableau.  A popular theory is that the word LAYeR could be formed when K4 is aligned with these raised positions, echoing Sanborn’s “Layer Two” hint for K2.  Again, this is conjecture, and there is no widely circulated paper demonstrating a valid decryption using this overlay.
	•	Matrix encryption and the extra‑“L” clue.  Cryptographers Craig Bauer, Gregory Link and Dante Molle proposed the “matrix encryption conjecture,” noting that the right‑hand Vigenère table on the sculpture contains an extra “L,” creating a 27‑character alphabet.  They speculated that this might point to a Hill‑type matrix cipher that blends the spatial layout of the tableau with the K4 ciphertext.  Their paper (published in Cryptologia in 2016) suggests testing linear algebraic transformations of the ciphertext against the tableau, but it ultimately concludes that no simple Hill cipher explains K4 and that further testing is needed.  Access to the full paper is limited, but it is referenced in summaries of Kryptos research .
	•	Meta‑instructions in the sculpture.  The CIA’s description of the sculpture notes that the right side is a flipped Vigenère table with one extra “L” and that Sanborn designed the fourth passage “to be very difficult to crack”  .  Sanborn has publicly released specific plaintext fragments (e.g., “BERLIN” and “CLOCK”) and confirmed that K4’s plaintext maps one‑to‑one with the ciphertext (no hidden characters or multi‑letter substitutions), but he has not hinted that 3‑D spatial analysis is required.

In summary, while the idea of overlaying K4 on the sculpture’s physical layout—perhaps even fitting a regression surface through the raised letters—is appealing, it remains largely a community hypothesis.  The most credible published research emphasizes that K4’s plaintext is still English (albeit “masked” to disrupt frequency analysis) , and no peer‑reviewed study has demonstrated that a three‑dimensional model or linear regression of the sculpture’s surface leads to a decryption.  Thus, using AI to incorporate spatial relationships into a cipher‑solving model would be novel territory, with very little prior work to build upon beyond enthusiast speculation .
